Step 1

Set up your ears

This lab plays one low sound into both ears and delays one ear by the time sound takes to travel around a human head. That delay is the interaural time difference (ITD). Below about 1,500 Hz it's the main cue your brain uses to tell left from right. Spend two minutes here before you listen.

Protect your hearing

  • Start with your device volume low and raise it slowly.
  • Low sounds seem quieter than they are. Don't turn the volume up to feel the bass. Your eardrums still take the full pressure.
  • Take a break every 10 to 15 minutes.
  • Stop right away if you feel pain, pressure in your ears, dizziness or nausea, or notice ringing afterwards.
  • If you have a hearing or balance condition, or recent ear surgery, ask a doctor before using this.
  • Don't use this while driving or anywhere you need to hear what's around you.
  • This is a learning tool. It isn't a hearing test and can't diagnose anything.
1

Set a safe volume

Turn your device volume down. Press the button to loop a soft 500 Hz tone at the page level. Raise the device volume slowly until the tone is quiet but clear, about as loud as someone speaking softly across a table.

  • After this, leave your device volume alone.
  • Use the Level slider at the top only for small changes.
  • Nothing in Explore plays at a higher signal level than this reference.
2

Check left and right

Each button beeps three times in one ear only.

  • Hear it in both ears? Turn off Mono Audio in your accessibility settings.
  • Sides reversed? Turn your headphones around.
  • Turn off spatial audio, head tracking, Dolby Atmos or Windows Sonic. They re-process stereo and change the timing.
  • Use headphones. With speakers, both ears hear both channels and the delay is lost.
3

Check your bass

These all play at the same level, so the lowest ones will sound quieter. That's normal.

  • Many earbuds fade out below 40 to 50 Hz. In the experiment, use frequencies you can hear clearly.
  • If a tone sounds buzzy or rattly, lower the Level. A buzz means distortion is adding higher frequencies, which are easier to locate and would spoil the test.

What to listen for

Where the sound should appear

Over headphones the sound usually sits inside your head rather than out in the room. At 0° it's in the middle. As you move toward 90°, it should slide toward the ear that hears it first.

Low versus higher frequencies

At 60 Hz one wave lasts about 17 ms, and the largest head delay of about 0.66 ms is only a 14° phase shift. At 400 Hz the same delay is a 94° shift. People are most sensitive near 500 to 1,000 Hz, where they can notice delays of about 10 to 20 µs.

Tone versus pulses

The pure tone fades in and out in both ears together, so only the phase of the wave differs. The rumble pulses also start earlier in one ear, which gives your brain a second clue. Compare them.

Front and back sound the same

A source at 30° and one at 150° make the same delay, so timing alone can't separate them. In real life you sort this out by moving your head and through the shape of your outer ears.

Only timing changes

Both ears always get the same level and the same tone colour. Below 400 Hz a real head barely changes either one. Direction cues, page 3, explains why.

How the delay is made

Your browser generates the sound live and delays one channel by a fraction of a sample, so the timing matches the model to well under a microsecond.

Background

How your brain finds direction

Three physical clues tell your brain which side a sound comes from. Below 400 Hz, the range this lab uses, one of them does nearly all the work.

In the sketches the sound is on your left, so the left ear (blue) is the near ear and the right ear (red) is the far ear.

Time difference

ITD · interaural time difference

The far ear's path is longer and wraps around your head, so the near ear hears the sound first. For a sound directly to one side the gap is about 0.66 ms. At low frequencies your brain reads it as a difference in the phase of the two waves.

Background

Diffraction around the head

A lens bends light because light travels at a different speed inside the glass. Sound mostly doesn't pass through your head at all. It diffracts, bending around the head. Waves much longer than your head (3.4 m at 100 Hz, 0.86 m at 400 Hz) bend around almost unchanged, so both ears get nearly the same level and tone colour. Waves a few centimetres long, at several kHz, are blocked, and the far ear sits in an acoustic shadow.

Two cues, one cause

Level and spectral differences both come from the head shadow. Measured at one frequency, the shadow shows up as a level difference. Across many frequencies, it shows up as a change in tone colour. A pure tone can only ever carry the level part: one frequency through any filter just comes out quieter and slightly shifted in time.

Duplex theory

In 1907 Lord Rayleigh proposed that we use timing for low sounds and level for high sounds, with a crossover around 1,500 Hz. It still holds as the broad picture. Below 400 Hz you're deep in timing territory.

Where phase stops working

The largest head delay, 0.66 ms, equals half a wave period at about 760 Hz. Above that, the brain can't tell whether a wave leads or lags. By about 1,500 Hz the nerve cells can no longer follow individual waves. In this lab's range the phase is never ambiguous: at 400 Hz the largest shift is 94°, well under 180°.

Inside your head or out in the room

On headphones, sounds made with timing alone usually sit inside your head, shifted toward one ear. Hearing a sound outside your head, at a distance, comes mostly from room echoes, small head movements and the filtering of your own outer ears. A source very close to your head, under about 1 m, also makes large level differences even at low frequencies, which the brain reads as nearby.

Front and back

A sound at 30° and one at 150° create the same time and level differences. All the directions that share a given difference form a cone around the line between your ears, called the cone of confusion. You resolve it by turning your head slightly and through outer-ear cues, which need frequencies above about 4 kHz.

Loudness compensation (A-weighting)

Your ears are much less sensitive to low frequencies. At the same signal level, a 400 Hz tone sounds far louder than a 50 Hz tone. A-weighting is the standard curve (IEC 61672) that describes this, based on how loud tones of different frequencies sound at a fairly quiet listening level, the 40-phon equal-loudness curve.

In Explore, frequencies above the anchor are turned down by their difference in A-weighting, so they sound about as loud as the anchor. Frequencies at or below the anchor play unchanged. For the rumble, each frequency inside the band gets its own correction.

The lab never turns anything up. Matching 20 Hz to the loudness of 400 Hz would take about 46 dB of extra bass, which could hurt your ears and your headphones.

A-weighting describes quiet to moderate listening. At louder levels your ear's response flattens, so the compensation can feel a little strong.
